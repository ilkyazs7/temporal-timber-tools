temporal timber: an overview

time-encoded material passports for circular design

01 the problem

architectural design is still largely material-agnostic. it assumes a steady supply of new resources, and treats materials as fixed specifications rather than as stock that ages.

buildings and their parts run on different clocks. structure, envelope and services age at different rates, and buildings are often taken down for functional, economic or regulatory reasons long before their materials fail. usable components are lost with them.

circular construction tools track, classify and reuse materials, but mostly as static records. they rarely account for degradation, exposure or remaining service life.

02 the approach

temporal timber embeds time as an operative design parameter. using cross-laminated timber (clt) as a case study, each element carries a material passport whose lifespan is not a fixed number but a condition: the result of its position, exposure, moisture and material properties.

the framework works at two scales: the element, described by its passport, and the building, where elements are reallocated through a rule-based substitution model.

03 the material passport

each passport joins geometric data from the model with material attributes:

  • identity · element id, zone id
  • position · zone, system, place in the building, distance to centre
  • geometry · dimensions, thickness, vertices
  • material · species, grade, installation year
  • risk · design risk conditions

a rule-based classification organises elements across global (zones, floors), element (wall, floor, façade) and local (relative position) scales. moisture is the primary variable: it drives dimensional change, decay and long-term structural performance, so lifespan decreases with exposure and distance from the driest point of the plan.

04 substitution

design intent and function are preserved: a wall stays a wall. replacements must maintain or improve the required performance: bending stiffness for floors and roofs, compressive strength for walls, density and durability at wall bases and high-risk zones. upgrades are allowed; downgrades are restricted.

06 demonstration

a single-storey clt building of 28 wall elements is followed over 60 years. lifespan decays with each element's position and moisture exposure. around year 40, a subset of walls falls below the building's target life: the bl > ml case.

those elements are replaced from an inventory of new, reclaimed and reconditioned panels, within the rules of the substitution matrix. the building's layout stays the same; the distribution of lifespans within it is recalibrated. elements stay in use within their viable range, and less new material is needed.

07 contributions

  1. 01 a method for encoding time-dependent attributes in material passports
  2. 02 a substitution framework that aligns material and building lifespans
  3. 03 an interface for inventory-based allocation of temporally differentiated elements
  4. 04 a demonstration of reducing temporal mismatch through targeted substitution

08 what comes next

optimisation for automated allocation, performance criteria beyond moisture and lifespan, and the upstream timeline of the material itself: from forest conditions to wood anatomy, lumber properties and clt performance.

beyond design, time-dependent material data could inform insurance, warranties, salvage and reuse, and construction finance, where decisions still rest on assumed lifespans. the architect's role may extend into long-term material stewardship.
